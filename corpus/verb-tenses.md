# Verb tenses and aspect

## Past simple vs present perfect

**Past simple** = a finished action in finished time. It answers *when*: *I
**saw** her yesterday.* *He **worked** there in 2019.*
**Present perfect** = a past action with present relevance, or unfinished time
up to now: *I **have seen** that film* (experience, no time given), *She **has
lived** here since 2019* (still true), *I **have lost** my keys* (I still can't
get in).

A time expression that closes the period forces the past simple: *yesterday,
last week, in 2019, ago, when I was young*. Never *I have seen him yesterday*.

American English often prefers the past simple where British English uses the
present perfect: *Did you eat yet?* / *Have you eaten yet?*

## since vs for

*since* + a point in time (*since Monday, since 2019, since I moved*);
*for* + a duration (*for three years, for ten minutes*). With a state that
continues to now, use the present perfect, **not** the present simple:
*I **have lived** here for three years* — not *I live here since three years*.

## Present perfect vs present perfect continuous

Simple emphasises the result or the number: *I **have written** three emails.*
Continuous emphasises the activity and its duration: *I **have been writing**
all morning.* Stative verbs (*know, be, have, own, believe, like*) do not take
the continuous: *I **have known** her for years*, never *have been knowing*.

## Past perfect

Use it only to make clear that one past event came **before** another: *The
train **had left** before we **arrived**.* With *before* or *after* the order is
already clear, so the past simple is also fine.

## Future

*will* = a decision made now, a prediction, a promise.
*going to* = an existing intention or evidence in the present: *Look at those
clouds — it **is going to** rain.*
Present continuous = a fixed arrangement: *I **am meeting** her at six.*
After *when, if, as soon as, until, before, after*, use the present, not *will*:
*I'll call you when I **arrive**.*
