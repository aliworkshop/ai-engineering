# Modifiers, word order and parallelism

## Dangling modifier

An introductory participial phrase attaches to the subject that follows it. If
that subject is not the thing doing the action, the modifier dangles.
✗ *Walking to the office, the rain started.* (the rain was not walking)
✓ *Walking to the office, I got caught in the rain.* / *As I walked to the
office, it started to rain.*

## Misplaced modifier

Put a modifier next to what it modifies. *Only* is the classic offender:
*Only I told her that* / *I only told her that* / *I told only her that* /
*I told her only that* all mean different things. Same with *almost, just,
even, nearly*.

## Squinting modifier

✗ *Reading quickly improves comprehension* — ambiguous. Move the adverb or
rewrite: *Reading improves comprehension quickly.*

## Adverb placement

Frequency adverbs (*always, often, never, usually, rarely*) go before a main
verb but after *be* and after the first auxiliary: *She **often** works late.*
*He is **always** late.* *I have **never** seen it.*
Never split a verb from its direct object with an adverb: ✗ *I like very much
this book.* ✓ *I like this book very much.*

## Parallelism

Items joined by *and, or, but* or by a correlative pair (*both…and, either…or,
neither…nor, not only…but also*) must share the same grammatical form.
✗ *She likes swimming, to cycle and hikes.*
✓ *She likes swimming, cycling and hiking.*
✗ *He is not only talented but also works hard.*
✓ *He is not only talented but also hardworking.*

## Word order basics

English is subject–verb–object and rarely allows anything between the verb and
its object. Adverbials follow the order **manner → place → time**: *She sang
beautifully at the concert last night.*
