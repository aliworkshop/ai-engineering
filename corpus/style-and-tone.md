# Style, register and clarity

Grammar decides whether a sentence is correct; style decides whether it is worth
reading. Correct these only when the writer has asked for more than correctness,
and always say which is which.

## Wordiness

Cut empty openers and padding: *There is / It is … that*, *due to the fact
that* (→ *because*), *in order to* (→ *to*), *at this point in time* (→ *now*),
*a large number of* (→ *many*), *has the ability to* (→ *can*).

## Passive voice

The passive is not an error. Prefer it when the doer is unknown, irrelevant or
deliberately hidden: *The samples were contaminated.* Prefer the active when the
doer matters: *The lab contaminated the samples.* A passive that buries
responsibility (*mistakes were made*) is a style problem, not a grammar one.

## Nominalisation

Verbs turned into nouns make sentences heavier: *We made a decision to conduct
an investigation* → *We decided to investigate.*

## Register

Contractions (*don't, we'll*), phrasal verbs (*find out, put off*) and *get* are
neutral-to-informal; their formal counterparts (*do not*, *discover*,
*postpone*, *become*) suit reports, applications and academic writing. Keep one
register throughout a piece.

## Hedging and confidence

Academic English hedges claims (*suggests, may indicate, is likely to*);
business English prefers directness. Stacked hedges (*it seems that it might
possibly be the case that*) weaken both.

## Sentence length and rhythm

Vary it. A run of 30-word sentences is exhausting; a run of 6-word sentences is
choppy. Break a sentence when it carries more than one idea.

## British and American English

*colour/color, centre/center, organise/organize, realise/realize,
travelled/traveled, licence (n)/license, practise (v)/practice, maths/math,
autumn/fall, flat/apartment.* Punctuation differs too: BrE puts the full stop
outside a closing quotation mark unless it belongs to the quote; AmE puts it
inside. Pick one variety and hold it.
