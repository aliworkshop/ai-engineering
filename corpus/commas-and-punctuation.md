# Commas and punctuation

## Comma splice

Two independent clauses cannot be joined by a comma alone.
✗ *It was raining, we stayed inside.*
Fix by (a) a full stop, (b) a semicolon, (c) a coordinating conjunction
(*for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*) after the comma, or (d) subordination:
*Because it was raining, we stayed inside.*

## Run-on (fused) sentence

Same problem with no punctuation at all: ✗ *It was raining we stayed inside.*

## When a comma is required

- Before *and, but, or, so, yet* joining two **independent** clauses: *She
  called, but nobody answered.* No comma when the second half is not a full
  clause: *She called and left a message.*
- After an introductory phrase or clause: *After the meeting, we went home.*
- Around non-defining (non-essential) relative clauses: *My brother, who lives
  in Rome, is a chef.* A defining clause takes **no** commas and prefers *that*:
  *The man **who** called is waiting.*
- Between items in a list. The final comma before *and* (the Oxford or serial
  comma) is optional but must be consistent; it prevents ambiguity: *my parents,
  Ayn Rand, and God.*
- Around interrupters and appositives: *The report, however, was late.*

## When a comma is wrong

Never between a subject and its verb (*The man in the grey coat, is my uncle*),
never before *that* in a defining clause, never before an indirect question.

## Semicolon vs colon

A **semicolon** joins two closely related independent clauses: *The deadline
passed; nobody noticed.* It also separates list items that already contain
commas. A **colon** introduces what follows — a list, an explanation or a quote
— and needs a complete clause before it: *We need three things: time, money and
staff.*

## Apostrophes

Possessive: *the dog's bowl* (singular), *the dogs' bowls* (plural ending in s),
*the children's toys* (irregular plural). *Its* = possessive; *it's* = *it is /
it has*. Plurals never take an apostrophe: *the 1990s, two DVDs.*
