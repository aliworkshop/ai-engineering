# Subject–verb agreement

The verb agrees with the **head noun** of the subject, not with the nearest noun.

- *The **box** of chocolates **is** on the table.* (head = box)
- *The **results** of the study **were** surprising.* (head = results)

## Cases that trip people up

**Intervening phrases.** Prepositional phrases, relative clauses and appositives
between the subject and the verb never change agreement: *A list of names **has**
been posted.*

**Indefinite pronouns.** *Each, every, either, neither, everyone, everybody,
somebody, nobody, anyone, one* take a singular verb: *Everyone **has** signed.*
*Neither of the answers **is** correct.* *Both, few, many, several* take a plural
verb. *All, any, most, none, some* follow the noun they refer to: *Most of the
water **is** gone* / *Most of the students **are** here.*

**Compound subjects.** Joined by *and* → plural (*Tom and Ana **are** here*),
unless the two names one thing: *Fish and chips **is** my favourite.* Joined by
*or / nor / either…or / neither…nor* → the verb agrees with the **nearer**
subject: *Neither the manager nor the employees **were** informed.*

**There is / there are.** The verb agrees with what follows: *There **are** three
options.* *There **is** one problem.*

**Collective nouns.** *Team, family, government, staff, committee* take a
singular verb in American English (*The team **is** winning*). British English
allows the plural when the members act individually (*The team **are**
arguing*).

**Quantities and units.** A measurement treated as one amount is singular:
*Ten kilometres **is** a long walk.* *Twenty dollars **was** stolen.*

**Nouns that look plural.** *News, mathematics, physics, economics, politics,
measles* are singular: *The news **is** good.*

**Titles and names.** *The Canterbury Tales **is** on the syllabus.*

**One of the … who/that.** The relative verb is plural — it refers to the plural
noun: *She is one of the students **who have** finished.* Compare *the only one
of the students **who has** finished.*
