# Conditionals

| Type | If-clause | Main clause | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zero | present simple | present simple | general truths: *If you heat ice, it melts.* |
| First | present simple | *will* + base | real future possibility: *If it rains, we **will** cancel.* |
| Second | past simple | *would* + base | unreal or unlikely present/future: *If I **had** more time, I **would** learn Greek.* |
| Third | past perfect | *would have* + past participle | unreal past: *If she **had left** earlier, she **would have caught** the train.* |

## The rule people break most often

Never put *will* or *would* in the *if*-clause of a first or second conditional.
✗ *If it **will** rain, we will cancel.* ✓ *If it rains, we will cancel.*
(*Will* is allowed in the if-clause only when it means willingness or a request:
*If you **will** wait a moment, I'll fetch her.*)

## Mixed conditionals

Past condition with a present result: *If I **had studied** medicine, I **would
be** a doctor now.*
Present condition with a past result: *If he **weren't** so careless, he
**wouldn't have lost** the keys.*

## were, not was

In the second conditional, formal English uses *were* for every person — the
subjunctive: *If I **were** you…* *If she **were** here, she would know.*
*Was* is common in speech but is marked as informal.

## unless, in case, as long as, provided that

*unless* = *if not*: *I won't go unless you come.* Do not add a second negative:
✗ *unless you don't come.*
*in case* = a precaution taken beforehand, not a condition: *Take an umbrella in
case it rains* (take it now, regardless).
*as long as / provided that / on condition that* introduce a stricter
condition than *if*.

## Inversion (formal)

*Had she known, she would have called.* = *If she had known…*
*Were I in your position, I would accept.* = *If I were…*
*Should you need help, call me.* = *If you should need help…*
